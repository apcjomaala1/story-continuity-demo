SOURCE TEXT: FANTASY ROMANCE ACADEMY SAMPLE

At Veyrfall Academy, the first bell of autumn did not ring. It breathed.

Blue fire unfurled from the bell tower above the tide cliffs, rolled over the slate roofs, and spilled toward the harbor where barges rocked under salt wind. The sound had color. It had weight. Students born to stormcraft tasted copper when it passed through them. Tidecraft students heard drowned stones answer from the water below. Liora Vale, who had never stood inside a school of magic before, felt stars open behind her eyes.

For one breath she saw the whole academy as if the old maps in parish books had become alive: seven terraces carved into black coastal rock, moonstone bridges strung between towers, and the Glassmere held upright along the western cliff by a spell older than the crown. The lake hung there like a silver wall. Fish swam sideways through it. Beyond its enchanted water, the real ocean struck the rocks so hard the arrival court trembled.

"Do not faint," said the girl beside her. "First impressions last longer than curses."

Liora tightened her grip on the handle of her cracked leather trunk. Her travel gown had been dyed green twice, and her dark braid was pinned with her mother's bone comb. "I was not going to faint."

"Everyone says that before they collapse on my boots." The girl offered a gloved hand. She had dark brown skin, sharp black eyes, and a silver crescent-blade signet at her throat. "Seraphine Mira Ardent. House Ardent. Blade-veil candidate, assuming the examiners were sober when they counted."

"Liora Elain Vale."

Seraphine's smile flickered with recognition. "From Bracken Parish?"

"Mostly by cart. Partly by mud."

Seraphine laughed, bright and dangerous, and the feather in her hat shivered in the sea wind. Liora had expected nobles to sneer at her parish dress and mended collar. Seraphine only looked amused by the mud, as if it had shown ambition.

The arrival court opened before them. At its center stood the Tide Gate, two arches of whale bone grown together and threaded with runes. The admission packet had warned Liora about the gate in red ink: it spoke legal names, admitted no one under a borrowed identity, and sealed after moonrise. The same packet had mentioned that the mainland bridge folded into the water during lockdown. Liora had thought that part was metaphor until she saw the bridge flex like a pale spine between the cliff road and shore.

"Next," called Master Iven Quill, the registrar, from beneath the gate.

Quill was thin enough to vanish behind his own ledger. Violet spectacles slid down his nose whenever he frowned, which seemed often. Each student stepped beneath the whale-bone arch while salt mist curled around their sleeves.

"Tamsin Roe of Halewater."

A freckled boy with webbed fingers hurried through.

"Jun Cader of Mourn."

A tall student with pale hair and a mourning ribbon crossed without expression.

"Seraphine Mira Ardent."

The gate tasted Seraphine's silver signet and let her pass.

Then Liora stepped beneath the arch.

The Tide Gate went quiet.

The gulls over the harbor seemed to hang in place. Liora felt those star-points behind her eyes again, quick and painful as sparks.

Master Quill lifted his pen. "State your full legal name."

"Liora Elain Vale."

The mist touched her cheek and brought with it the scent of lavender soap. For a heartbeat she was back in the cottage kitchen at Bracken Parish while Elowen Vale braided her hair before market. Her mother had always worked with herb bundles hanging from the rafters and star charts hidden under the flour sacks. When Liora was small, Elowen had laughed and called the charts old weather.

The gate spoke.

"Liora Elain Vale."

Then, in a second voice beneath the first, it added, "Daughter of the sealed star."

Whispers moved through the court.

Liora turned toward Seraphine, but the crowd had already parted for someone else.

Prince Caelan Aer Thorne crossed the Tide Gate without waiting for Quill to call him. The arch flared silver around him.

"Caelan Aer Thorne," the gate declared, "Crown Prince of Merrowyn, heir to the Storm Seat, stormcraft candidate, royal ward of Veyrfall."

Every student bowed.

Liora realized a breath too late that she should do the same. By the time she dipped, Caelan had seen her standing alone above the lowered heads. He was taller than the portraits in the parish chapel, dark-haired like the queen, with gray eyes that made Liora think of lightning trapped beneath winter ice. The black signet on his right hand caught the blue fire still fading from the bell.

He passed without speaking, but every lantern in the court leaned toward him.

"You may enjoy danger, Vale," Seraphine murmured as she tugged Liora upright, "but I enjoy breakfast."

The first-years were herded up Scholars' Walk to the Hall of Lanterns. Veyrfall rose around them with impossible confidence: the Obsidian Spire above the court, the copper-roofed dueling hall, the greenhouse domes where carnivorous roses pressed petals against the glass, and East Dormitory clinging to the cliff above the lower orchards. Across a covered bridge stood the old storm tower where animated armor waited in the shadows. Caelan's rooms were there, Seraphine whispered, because princes required guards even when they hated them.

The Hall of Lanterns was vast enough to hold weather. Hundreds of glass lamps floated under the ceiling, each carrying a different flame: foxfire blue, witchlight green, sunshard gold, ember red, and one dark flame that drank brightness from everything near it.

Headmistress Ysilde Veyr sat beneath the lanterns with a caged star hovering beside her shoulder. She was old in the way cliffs were old, weathered into certainty. Professor Mael, broad-shouldered and silver-bearded, occupied the stormcraft master's chair with the relaxed cheer of a man who considered lightning a breakfast food. Professor Root had leaves tucked into her sleeves. Master Quill stood beside the ledgers, already scowling at a blot of ink.

"First-years," Headmistress Veyr said, and the hall quieted. "You have crossed the Tide Gate. You have been named, measured, and admitted. Veyrfall will teach you to command power without being consumed by appetite, fear, ambition, or love."

Older students laughed softly.

Veyr waited until the sound died. "Some of you believe love is harmless. Those students will provide examples for the rest."

Seraphine leaned close. "She says that every year. My brother wrote it in the margin of his admission letter before he got engaged to a duelist and burned a chapel roof."

The rankings came after the welcome.

"First seat: Mara Venn, scholarship student, artificer candidate, unaligned."

Mara Venn stood on the fountain rim below the dais, copper hair cropped to her jaw and muddy boots planted among carved lilies. A brass mechanical kestrel perched on her shoulder. It clicked, spread one articulated wing, and stole the shine from a nearby noble's cuff button.

"That is Pip," Seraphine whispered. "He steals shiny things and regrets nothing."

"Second seat: Dorian Mourn, veilcraft candidate, House Mourn."

Dorian Mourn stepped forward with silver-blond hair tied at his nape and eyes pale enough to look colorless. House Mourn's crest, a white moth above a closed eye, glimmered at his cuff. He bowed as if emotion were a provincial habit.

"Third seat: Caelan Thorne, stormcraft candidate, Crown House."

Caelan did not look toward the galleries. He stood in the space the other nobles left around him and seemed lonelier than anyone with that much attention had a right to be.

"Fourth seat: Seraphine Ardent, blade-veil candidate, House Ardent."

Seraphine smiled as if fourth place had arrived precisely on schedule.

"Fifth seat: Liora Vale, star-reading candidate, unaligned."

The hall turned toward Liora. She felt the weight of every title she did not have: no house, no patron, no court manners, no guard at her shoulder. The caged star by the headmistress turned too, and its light found the bone comb in her braid.

Supper was served in a chamber carved into the cliff. Windows looked into the Glassmere, where silver fish drifted past the panes and the ocean foamed beyond them. Star-reading candidates received clear broth in black bowls, each bowl holding tiny lights. Mara leaned across the table and announced that it looked like someone had boiled the night.

"Do not swallow the lights," Dorian said.

Mara's hand froze an inch from Liora's bowl. "For research?"

"For nightmares."

Liora looked toward the high table. An empty chair stood beside Headmistress Veyr, draped in white cloth.

"Who is that for?" she asked.

Seraphine's teasing softened. "Elowen Vale."

The name struck Liora so hard she set down her spoon.

"My mother?"

Dorian went still. "You did not know?"

"My mother was an herbalist."

"Your mother was Veyrfall's last master of star-reading," Dorian said. "She sealed the North Observatory sixteen years ago and vanished."

Liora stared at the white-draped chair. Elowen had died of marsh fever when Liora was twelve. Liora had buried her behind the cottage rowan tree. She remembered cold hands smelling faintly of lavender. She remembered the parish priest's record. She remembered grief too clearly for it to be a mistake.

Across the chamber, Caelan Thorne stood from the stormcraft table. Conversation thinned around him as he walked to Liora.

"Vale," he said.

"Your Highness."

"Caelan, here." He placed an iron key on the table. A storm-blue ribbon was threaded through it, and the teeth were shaped like lightning. "Your mother gave this to my father the night she vanished. He told me to return it if a Vale crossed the Tide Gate."

Liora did not touch it. "My mother died five years ago."

"Then someone lied to both of us."

The words were quiet. They still seemed to reach every table.

Liora's star-sense stirred around the iron. She saw frost on a tower door, a woman's hand closing around the same key, and heard Elowen singing a Bracken Parish lullaby backward.

"The North Observatory opens at first frost," Caelan said. "If the key is yours, it will answer you then."

"And if it is not?"

"It will burn you."

"Veyrfall has a limited vocabulary."

For the first time, his mouth almost softened. "Yes."

That night, Liora unpacked in East Dormitory. Seraphine had the room to her left. Mara had the room to her right; sparks leaked under the door whenever Pip objected to maintenance. Dorian's room waited at the far end of the corridor behind pale ward-light. Across the covered bridge, the storm tower windows burned blue.

Liora had just placed the iron key on her desk when someone knocked.

Caelan stood in the corridor without guards, jacket unbuttoned, hair wind-tossed from the bridge.

"You should not be here," Liora said.

"Because of propriety or politics?"

"Both, and possibly architecture. Does the armor know you left?"

"The armor owes me three favors."

"That raises more questions than it answers."

He glanced down the hall. Seraphine's door was open by a suspicious finger-width. "My father made me swear an oath on the Storm Seat. If the sealed star returned to Veyrfall, I would keep it from opening the North Observatory."

"By sealed star, you mean me."

"I did not know that then."

"But you know now."

"Yes."

Liora looked at the key on her desk. "Your warning at supper was incomplete."

"Yes."

The honesty annoyed her more than a lie might have.

Footsteps sounded below. Caelan stepped back, but Liora caught his sleeve before he could leave. The moment her fingers touched the fabric, her star-sense opened.

She saw lightning chains around his heart. She saw a boy at thirteen swearing by blood before a throne carved from a fallen star. She saw him older, kneeling in a ruined chapel with her name on his lips. She saw herself kissing him beneath falling ash and then turning away from him at a door full of stars.

She let go.

Caelan's gray eyes had widened. "What did you see?"

"Nothing useful."

"Liora."

It was the first time he said her name. No title. No caution. The sound unsettled her more than the vision.

Seraphine opened her door the rest of the way. "Your Highness. How refreshing to discover the storm tower has developed a hallway."

Caelan straightened. "Ardent."

"Vale needs sleep. You need witnesses who are not me."

"I was leaving."

"Excellent. We agree on something."

After he crossed back to the storm tower, Seraphine leaned against Liora's doorframe. "I had prepared at least twelve remarks."

"Burn them."

"Several are complimentary."

"Especially burn those."

Before dawn, East Dormitory screamed.

Liora ran into the corridor with Seraphine, Mara, Pip, and Dorian. On the landing below, a white wall had split open. Red letters oozed from the stone as if the building itself were bleeding.

RETURN THE SEALED STAR OR THE PRINCE DROWNS.

The letters shivered and reformed.

FIRST FROST COMES EARLY.

"Dormitory walls bleed only on founding anniversaries," Dorian said, voice tight.

"You said anniversaries were real?" Liora demanded.

"Statistically."

Caelan arrived barefoot from the storm tower, lightning flickering over his hands. Headmistress Veyr's voice moved through the stone before anyone could speak.

"All students remain where you are. Prefects secure the bridges. Professors to East Dormitory."

By sunrise, Veyrfall locked every gate. The mainland bridge folded itself into the water. Boats stayed in harbor. The academy remained cut off on its cliff above the sea, wrapped in salt wind and upward-falling snow.

Classes began anyway.

"Tradition," Seraphine said as they crossed the frosted courtyard. "Veyrfall could sink and still assign reading."

"If it sank, tidecraft scores would improve," Mara said, walking backward while adjusting Pip's brass wing.

"Some of us dislike drowning."

"Skill issue."

Professor Mael opened Practical Dangers by pointing at Caelan. "Attack me."

Stormlight gathered around Caelan's right hand. He released blue-white lightning straight at the professor's chest.

Mael caught it in his teeth.

The class recoiled.

He swallowed. Smoke drifted from his ears. "The first rule of power: someone is always better at it than you."

Mara whispered, "I adore him."

"You would," Dorian said.

When Liora's turn came, Professor Mael placed a black bowl of star-water before her. "Look and name the threat closest to you."

The water showed stars instead of the ceiling. Liora saw Seraphine laughing under a blood-red moon with tears on her face. She saw Mara's brass kestrel flying through smoke with one wing missing. She saw Dorian holding a white wax letter and deciding not to warn her. She saw Caelan standing at the edge of the Glassmere while water rose around his ankles, though the lake hung vertical beside him.

"The closest threat is choice," Liora said.

The bowl cracked in half.

By noon she had three invitations from noble houses, two anonymous warnings, and a folded star-note that read: Ask the prince what he promised.

She found Caelan in the west orchard before sunset. Black apples hung from the branches, their silver flesh visible where birds had pecked the skins. An animated suit of armor followed him between the trees until he dismissed it with a single look.

"What did you promise?" Liora asked.

Caelan burned the note with stormlight. "At thirteen, I swore to keep the sealed star from opening the North Observatory."

"Are you going to keep your oath?"

He looked at her for a long moment. "I do not know."

"That is a terrible answer."

"It is the only one I have that is not a lie."

The orchard gate slammed shut. Frost raced across the iron bars though the sun stood high. A figure stepped out of the bark of the oldest tree, robed in old academy black with star-silver cuffs and a face hidden by white wax.

"Elowen's daughter," it said. "Open what she sealed, or watch the prince drown in air."

"Everyone keeps asking me to open doors no one will explain," Liora said.

"She has friends," Seraphine called from the orchard wall.

Mara crouched beside her, Pip's brass wings spread. Dorian emerged from a shadow near the gate.

"Friends is an ambitious term," Dorian said. "Witnesses with recurring proximity."

"He means friends," Mara said.

The wax-veiled figure dissolved into frost that wrapped around Caelan's throat. He gasped. Lightning struck every tree at once.

Liora took his hand.

Names mattered at Veyrfall. The Tide Gate knew them. Oaths bent around them. The old magic listened when they were spoken together.

"Liora Vale," she said. "Caelan Thorne."

The frost ring shattered.

Caelan fell against her, alive and furious and breathing.

"You broke part of the oath," he said.

"Good."

"It will punish you."

"It can get in line."

Headmistress Veyr summoned them to the Obsidian Spire. Seraphine, Mara, Pip, and Dorian came anyway, because none of them were good at leaving danger to authorized people.

At the top of the spire, the caged star burned beside the headmistress.

"Seventeen years ago," Veyr said, "Elowen Vale discovered that the founding spell had been altered. The Glassmere, the Tide Gate, the discipline wards, and the royal oaths tied to the Storm Seat all draw from the same old bargain. We call it the Starless Compact because the founders removed its true name from the sky."

Dorian's face sharpened. "Who altered it?"

"Someone inside Veyrfall. Someone with access to founding law, royal blood, and star-reading."

"And my mother?" Liora asked.

"Elowen sealed the North Observatory before the altered Compact could use Caelan's death as a key. Then she fled with you. The body buried in Bracken Parish may have been a mercy-double grown from rowan and memory. I believe Elowen lives, but I do not know where."

Liora's grief changed shape without leaving.

"No one opens the Observatory through his death," she said. "No one uses my mother's work without telling me the price. And if Veyrfall wants my help, it stops treating me like a rumor with boots."

Headmistress Veyr inclined her head. "A reasonable beginning."

Far above them, though no visible tower rose higher than the spire, a bell rang from a place that should not exist. Once. Twice. Seven times.

The North Observatory had heard her.

Caelan took Liora's hand in front of the headmistress, the caged star, and the friends who would not leave.

This time, neither of them pretended it was an accident.

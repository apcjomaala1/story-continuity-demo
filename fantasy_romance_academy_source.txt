SOURCE TEXT: FANTASY ROMANCE ACADEMY SAMPLE

At Veyrfall Academy, the first bell of autumn did not ring. It breathed.

The sound unfurled from the tower above the tide cliffs in a ribbon of blue fire, crossed the slate roofs, skimmed the orchard walls, and slipped into the harbor where the anchored barges turned their lanterns toward shore. Every student in the incoming class felt it differently. Those born to stormcraft tasted copper. Those with river magic heard the voices of drowned stones. Those with no gift at all only felt a cold pressure behind the ribs, as if the bell had reached into them and checked whether their courage was properly laced.

Liora Vale felt stars.

They opened behind her eyes, a hundred tiny wounds of white light, each one paired with a name she did not yet know. For one breath she saw the whole academy as the old maps drew it: seven terraces cut into black coastal rock, bridges of moonstone laid between towers, salt gardens flashing silver below, and the Glassmere, a lake held upright along the western cliff by a spell older than the monarchy. The lake hung there like a polished wall, catching the morning sun, its fish swimming sideways through suspended water. The sight vanished before she could gasp.

"Do not faint," said the girl beside her. "First impressions last longer than curses."

Liora tightened her grip on the handle of her trunk. The leather was cracked, the brass corners green with age. It had belonged to her mother before it belonged to her, and before that to no one respectable. "I was not going to faint."

"Everyone says that right before they collapse on my boots." The girl offered a gloved hand with a courtly tilt of the chin that made the feather in her hat tremble. She had dark brown skin, sharp black eyes, and a silver signet at her throat shaped like a crescent blade. "Seraphine Ardent. House Ardent if you care about that sort of thing, which you should, because everyone else does."

"Liora Vale."

Seraphine's smile flickered. Not cruelly. Carefully. "From the north road?"

"From Bracken Parish."

"Then you came by cart."

"Mostly."

"And partly by mud, judging from your hem."

Liora looked down. The mud had dried in honest brown streaks across her travel dress. Around them, the other first-years wore academy blacks tailored with house colors threaded at the cuffs: sun-gold for Auric, ash-gray for Mourn, river-blue for Halewater, blade-silver for Ardent. Liora wore the parish best, dyed green twice to hide fading, with a collar she had mended in thread too pale for the cloth. She lifted her chin anyway. "The mud was very determined."

Seraphine laughed, and that was how Liora met her first friend at Veyrfall, though she did not know yet that friendship at the academy could be bartered, tested, or made illegal by winter.

The tide cliffs rose behind the arrival court like the backs of sleeping giants. Above them, Veyrfall Academy leaned into the sea wind with impossible confidence. Its oldest tower, the Obsidian Spire, was blacker than night and windowless except for a single round aperture near the top where the headmistress kept a star burning in a glass cage. The newer buildings had been added by wealthier centuries: the white Scholars' Walk with its carved saints of logic, the copper-roofed dueling hall, the greenhouse domes where carnivorous roses pressed petals against the glass, and the East Dormitory, whose balconies were crowded with returning students pretending not to inspect the new arrivals.

At the center of the court stood the Tide Gate, two arches of whale bone grown together and threaded with runes. No one could enter the academy proper until the gate spoke their legal name. No one could leave without permission after moonrise. Liora had read that rule in the admission packet twelve times. It was printed in red ink beneath the academy motto: The heart remembers what power forgets.

"Next," called Master Iven Quill, the registrar, from beneath the gate.

He was a narrow man in a violet coat, with spectacles that kept sliding down a nose too long for patience. Each time a student stepped forward, the Tide Gate breathed salt mist and pronounced a name in a voice like waves against hollow stone.

"Tamsin Roe of Halewater."

A freckled boy with webbed fingers bowed and hurried through.

"Jun Cader of Mourn."

A tall student with pale hair and a mourning ribbon crossed without expression.

"Seraphine Mira Ardent."

Seraphine winked at Liora before stepping beneath the arch. The mist curled around her gloves, tasted the silver blade at her throat, and let her pass. On the other side, a prefect in silver-trimmed black handed her a room token.

Liora stepped forward when Master Quill beckoned.

The Tide Gate went quiet.

Not the ordinary pause of a spell searching a ledger. The entire arrival court tightened. Even the gulls wheeling above the harbor seemed to hang in place. Liora felt the star-sense prickle again, bright points behind her eyes. The gate's runes glowed, failed, then glowed a second time in colors she had no names for.

Master Quill peered over his spectacles. "State your full name."

"Liora Elain Vale."

The mist touched her cheeks. It smelled of salt, iron, and the lavender soap her mother had made on Sundays. For a heartbeat Liora was six years old again, standing in a cottage kitchen while Elain Vale plaited her hair and warned her that magic always had teeth.

The gate spoke.

"Liora Elain Vale."

Then, softer, in a second voice beneath the first: "Daughter of the sealed star."

Whispers spread through the court like spilled ink.

Master Quill's quill snapped in his hand.

Liora's mouth went dry. "Is that bad?"

"No student questions the gate," Master Quill said too quickly. He wiped ink from his fingers. "Proceed."

She passed beneath the whale-bone arch. Cold mist slid under her skin, and the academy accepted her with the intimate discomfort of a hand closing around her wrist.

On the far side, Seraphine waited with two room tokens instead of one. "Interesting."

"I prefer boring."

"Then you came to the wrong school."

Before Liora could answer, the crowd parted with the precision of a knife dividing silk.

Prince Caelan Thorne had arrived.

He did not look like the portraits in the parish hall. The portraits showed a boy with gold hair, a ceremonial sword, and a smile painted by someone who had never met a teenage monarch's son. The real Caelan was taller than expected, dark-haired like the queen, and unadorned except for the black signet on his right hand. His uniform fit him without effort. His expression did not.

He came through the Tide Gate without waiting for Master Quill to call his name. The arch flared silver, and its voice rang across the court.

"Caelan Aer Thorne, Crown Prince of Merrowyn, heir to the Storm Seat, ward of Veyrfall."

No second voice followed. No hidden title. No embarrassing prophecy. Everyone bowed anyway.

Liora did not. Not because she meant to be brave. She had never bowed to anyone except old Mistress Pell at chapel, and she had been taught that princes in portraits could not see you. By the time she realized the entire court had lowered around her, Caelan's gray eyes had already found hers.

For one suspended moment, Liora saw lightning trapped under ice.

Then Seraphine hooked a hand around Liora's elbow and tugged her down. "You may enjoy danger, Vale, but I enjoy breakfast."

Caelan passed them without a word. The wind shifted as he moved, and every candle in every arrival lantern bent toward him.

"He is in our year?" Liora whispered.

"Technically he is above everyone by birth and below several people by temperament," Seraphine said. "But yes. Third seat in the first-year cohort, because royal tutors do not count for academy advancement."

"Third seat?"

"Rankings are announced tonight. The academy orders students by tested strength, political weight, and headmistress whim. It pretends only the first two matter."

Liora looked toward Caelan, who had stopped near the courtyard fountain. A group of noble students formed around him at a careful distance, each eager to be noticed, none daring to stand too close. He seemed lonelier than anyone with that much attention had a right to be.

"Who is first seat?"

Seraphine pointed toward the fountain.

Not at Caelan.

At the girl standing on the rim of it.

She had cropped copper hair, boots muddy enough to rival Liora's, and a grin sharp enough to cut ribbon. A mechanical kestrel perched on her shoulder, its wings folded in brass plates. While the surrounding nobles whispered about rank and lineage, she was feeding biscuit crumbs to a fountain spirit that looked like a translucent child made of rain.

"Mara Venn," Seraphine said. "Scholarship student. Artificer. Broke the south testing hall during examinations and repaired it into a better testing hall before anyone could expel her."

Mara noticed them looking and raised two fingers in greeting. The mechanical kestrel mimicked the gesture with one brass talon.

"I thought scholarship students did not take first seat."

"They do when the test crystal catches fire and starts applauding."

Liora was still staring when a shadow fell across her boots.

"You are Vale," said a voice like velvet used to conceal a blade.

The speaker was a boy with silver-blond hair tied back at his nape, eyes so pale they looked almost colorless, and the flawless posture of someone raised among mirrors and enemies. His uniform carried ash-gray thread at the cuffs. House Mourn. The old funeral dukes. Liora knew their crest from history primers: a white moth above a closed eye.

"Yes," she said.

"Dorian Mourn." He did not offer his hand. "Your name startled the gate."

"It startled me too."

"That suggests honesty or poor preparation. Neither survives long here."

Seraphine's smile cooled. "Careful, Dorian. Some of us like our first conversations without autopsy notes."

Dorian looked at her. "And some of us prefer knowing whether a sealed-star daughter has been placed in our dormitory before the walls begin bleeding."

Liora blinked. "Do walls do that?"

"Only in East Dormitory," Seraphine said.

"Only on anniversaries," Dorian corrected.

Mara Venn hopped down from the fountain and strode over before anyone could explain whether they were joking. The fountain spirit clung to her sleeve, leaking water on the stones. "You lot are gloomy. Hello. I am Mara. If your trunk bites, bring it to me before midnight. After midnight I charge."

"My trunk does not bite."

"Yet. Veyrfall furniture is impressionable."

Mara held out a grease-stained hand. Liora shook it and felt a tiny spark jump between their fingers. The brass kestrel clicked, extended its head, and inspected Liora's hairpin.

"This is Pip," Mara said. "He steals shiny things but regrets nothing."

Pip snapped his beak at Dorian's signet.

Dorian stepped back. "If that device touches me, I will have it melted."

"He bites people who threaten him."

"Good," Seraphine said. "We have something in common."

The first bell breathed again, shorter this time. Prefects began herding students toward the Scholars' Walk. Liora let herself be pulled along with Seraphine, Mara, and the reluctant orbit of Dorian Mourn. Ahead of them, Prince Caelan walked alone, the space around him kept empty by habit and fear.

The Scholars' Walk climbed from the arrival court through a corridor open to the sea. On one side, waves struck the cliff below in bursts of white. On the other, statues of the academy's founders watched the students pass. Saint Orra of the Unburning Library held a book chained shut. Admiral Veyr, who had raised the first tower from volcanic glass, held a broken compass. Queen Sabeth the Practical held a ledger and a dagger, because the queen had funded the school only after the headmistress of her day proved that educated mages paid higher taxes.

"Veyrfall trains five disciplines," Seraphine recited, clearly enjoying the chance to sound bored by knowledge. "Stormcraft, tidecraft, emberwork, veilcraft, and star-reading. The official curriculum says all are equal. The unofficial curriculum says stormcraft wins duels, veilcraft wins scandals, and star-reading gets you blamed for things that have not happened yet."

"I did not choose star-reading," Liora said.

Dorian gave a thin smile. "No one with sense does."

Mara leaned around Seraphine. "Can you really see futures?"

"No. I see..." Liora hesitated. The honest answer sounded mad. "Connections, sometimes. Points of light. Names. The shape of a place."

"Useful."

"Frightening," Dorian said.

"Both can be true." Mara touched Pip's brass head. "Every proper invention begins by making someone nervous."

The corridor ended at the Hall of Lanterns, a chamber vast enough to hold weather. Hundreds of glass lamps floated under the vaulted ceiling, each containing a different flame: blue foxfire, green witchlight, red ember, gold sunshard, and a single dark flame that drank light instead of giving it. Students gathered beneath them by cohort. Returning students filled the upper galleries. Professors stood on the dais in high-backed chairs carved from driftwood and bone.

At the center chair sat Headmistress Ysilde Veyr.

She was old in the way mountains were old: not frail, not softened, but worn into certainty. Her white hair fell in a long braid over a black robe clasped with a piece of raw obsidian. A star burned in a glass cage beside her shoulder, the same star Liora had seen from the tower aperture. It made no heat. Its light brushed every face in the hall and seemed to remember each one.

"First-years," said the headmistress.

The hall quieted at once.

"You have crossed the Tide Gate. You have been named, measured, and admitted. For the next four years, Veyrfall will teach you to command power without being consumed by appetite, fear, ambition, or love."

A ripple of laughter moved through the older students. The headmistress waited until it died.

"Some of you believe love is not a danger. Those students will provide examples for the rest."

Seraphine murmured, "She says that every year."

"How do you know?"

"My brother wrote it in the margin of his admission letter. Then he got engaged to a duelist and set a chapel roof on fire."

The headmistress lifted one hand. Five banners unfurled behind her: a storm cloud pierced by a crown, a silver tide, a black candle, a mirrored eye, and a seven-pointed star. "Your disciplines will be assessed by winter. Your houses will not save you from failure. Your families will not purchase your examinations. Your titles will open doors, and the academy will put teeth behind those doors. Enter knowingly."

As if summoned by the warning, the dark flame overhead pulsed.

Liora's vision doubled.

She saw the hall as it was: lamps, banners, faces turned upward. She saw it as it had been two hundred years earlier: flooded to the second gallery, students standing on tables while a woman in a red coat sang the sea backward. She saw it as it might be: broken glass underfoot, Caelan Thorne reaching for her with blood on his sleeve, Seraphine screaming a name that had been erased from every book, Mara's brass kestrel flying through smoke with one wing missing, Dorian Mourn kneeling before an open door full of stars.

Then Caelan looked back.

Their eyes met across the hall, and the vision snapped into one clear image: his hand around hers beneath a burning sky.

Liora inhaled too sharply.

The star in the headmistress's cage turned toward her.

Headmistress Veyr's gaze followed.

"Liora Elain Vale," she said.

Every head turned.

Liora wanted the floor to open. Veyrfall floors were probably capable of it.

"Step forward."

Seraphine squeezed her wrist once before releasing her. Mara mouthed something that might have been "good luck" or "do not explode." Dorian looked frankly fascinated.

Liora walked to the dais with the entire academy watching. Her boots sounded too loud on the black stone. When she stopped before the headmistress, the caged star brightened until the glass hummed.

"You carry an old inheritance," Headmistress Veyr said quietly enough that only the professors and nearest students could hear. "Did your mother teach you its name?"

Liora swallowed. "My mother taught me herb prices, parish songs, and how to hide silver under flour sacks."

"But not its name."

"No."

"Then learn quickly."

The headmistress stood. Her voice carried again. "The rankings for the first-year cohort are as follows. First seat: Mara Venn, artificer candidate, unaligned. Second seat: Dorian Mourn, veilcraft candidate, House Mourn. Third seat: Caelan Thorne, stormcraft candidate, Crown House. Fourth seat: Seraphine Ardent, blade-veil candidate, House Ardent."

Seraphine made a pleased sound as if fourth were exactly what she had ordered.

"Fifth seat," the headmistress said, and looked down at Liora. "Liora Vale, star-reading candidate, unaligned."

The hall erupted.

Unaligned students did not place in the first five twice in one year. Parish girls did not enter above sons of dukes. Star-readers did not rank highly before formal testing because their gifts were notoriously difficult to measure and notoriously easy to fear.

Liora stood still under the noise. She felt not triumph, but the shape of a target settling between her shoulder blades.

From the third row, Caelan Thorne watched her with an expression she could not read.

When the ceremony ended, the first-years were sent to supper beneath the Hall of Lanterns, in a dining chamber carved directly into the cliff. Windows looked out into the suspended wall of the Glassmere. Fish drifted past the panes, silver bellies flashing. At certain angles, Liora could see the sea beyond the lake and the lake beyond the sea, water layered against water by impossible force.

Food appeared in courses according to discipline. Emberwork tables received stews that smoked blue. Tidecraft tables received fish that changed flavor when salted. Stormcraft students were served cloud bread, crisp outside and full of thunder-soft air. Star-reading candidates, of whom there were only nine across all years, received black bowls of clear broth in which tiny lights floated.

"That looks like someone boiled the night," Mara said, leaning across the table.

Liora nudged the bowl with her spoon. "Is it safe?"

Dorian, who had sat opposite them despite claiming to prefer silence, said, "Nothing here is safe. The broth is traditional. It teaches restraint."

"How?"

"If you swallow a light, you dream someone else's memory."

Mara immediately reached for the bowl.

Liora pulled it away. "No."

"For research."

"Still no."

Seraphine cut into a pear glazed with winter honey. "Let Vale keep her strangers' memories. She has collected enough attention for one day."

"Attention finds blood in the water," Dorian said.

"You are cheerful as a funeral invoice."

"Funerals are one of my family's core industries."

At the high table, professors spoke behind goblets and glances. Master Quill had replaced his snapped pen and seemed determined not to look at Liora. Professor Mael, the stormcraft master, laughed loudly at something Caelan did not say. Professor Annis Root, keeper of botanical dangers, fed crumbs to a vine curling from her sleeve. Beside the headmistress sat an empty chair with no place setting, draped in white cloth.

"Who is that for?" Liora asked.

The others followed her gaze.

Seraphine's expression dimmed. "The missing professor. Elowen Vale."

The name struck like a thrown stone.

Liora set down her spoon. "My mother?"

Dorian went very still. Mara's grin vanished.

Seraphine looked between them. "You did not know?"

"My mother was an herbalist."

"Your mother was Veyrfall's last master of star-reading," Dorian said. "She disappeared sixteen years ago after sealing the North Observatory. Everyone knows that."

"I am seventeen."

No one answered.

The suspended lake pressed silver light through the window. Liora stared at the empty chair until its white cloth blurred. Her mother had died of marsh fever when Liora was twelve. That was what the parish priest recorded. That was what Liora had buried beneath a rowan tree behind the cottage. There had been a body. There had been lavender soap on cold hands. There had been no mention of Veyrfall, no sealed observatory, no missing professor.

"Everyone knows wrong," Liora said.

But her voice had no weight.

Across the chamber, Caelan Thorne stood abruptly from the stormcraft table. His chair scraped stone. A hush opened around him. He walked not to the doors, nor to the high table, but to Liora.

Seraphine muttered, "Oh, this is splendidly terrible."

Caelan stopped beside Liora's chair. Up close, he looked tired. Not bored, not arrogant. Tired in a way that made his youth seem like a badly kept secret.

"Vale," he said.

"Your Highness."

"Do not call me that here."

Several nearby nobles flinched as if she had insulted him.

Liora lifted her chin. "What should I call you?"

"Caelan."

Dorian's eyebrows rose. Seraphine hid a smile behind her cup. Mara did not bother hiding anything.

Caelan placed something on the table: a small iron key threaded on a storm-blue ribbon. Its teeth were shaped like lightning. "Your mother gave this to my father the night she vanished. He told me to return it if a Vale ever crossed the Tide Gate."

Liora did not touch it. "My mother is dead."

"Then someone lied to both of us."

The words landed softly, but every conversation within three tables stopped.

Liora looked at the key. Her star-sense stirred. In the iron she saw a tower door, frost on the handle, a woman's voice singing an old parish lullaby backward, and Caelan at ten years old hiding behind a curtain while two adults argued about a child who must never be found.

She snatched her hand back before touching it.

Caelan noticed. "You saw something."

"I saw that I should eat my very strange soup and sleep for a week."

"The North Observatory opens at first frost," he said. "If the key is yours, it will answer you then."

"And if it is not?"

"Then it will burn you."

"Comforting."

For the first time, his mouth almost softened. Not a smile. The possibility of one. "Veyrfall has a limited vocabulary."

He turned to leave, but Liora spoke before caution could stop her. "Why did you bring it in front of everyone?"

Caelan looked back. The suspended lake cast moving light across his face. "Because secrets here rot faster in darkness."

"That sounds like something someone says before keeping five of them."

This time he did smile, brief and unwilling. "Probably."

He returned to his table.

Seraphine leaned close. "You just accused the crown prince of hypocrisy."

"Did I?"

"In a charming rustic way."

Dorian reached for the key, but stopped before touching it. "Iron, storm-bound, pre-war work. Worth more than your village."

"Do not touch it."

"I did not."

"You thought loudly."

Mara propped her chin in her hands. "I like her."

After supper, prefects led the first-years across moonlit bridges to East Dormitory. The building clung to the cliff face above the lower orchards, its stone walls veined with pale quartz. The sea breathed below. Lantern moths drifted through the air, each one carrying a spark of borrowed candlelight. Somewhere in the gardens, a bell-elk cried, and the sound made the windows tremble.

Liora's room was on the seventh floor, which the prefect described as "traditionally unlucky, but only in a statistical sense." Seraphine had the room to her left. Mara had the room to her right. Dorian had a room at the far end of the corridor, a placement Seraphine called evidence of divine mercy. Caelan, as royal ward, was housed in the old storm tower attached to the dormitory by a covered bridge patrolled by animated suits of armor.

"The armor moves?" Liora asked as the prefect handed her a brass keycard.

"Only if threatened, insulted, or bored," said the prefect. "Do not give it poetry."

Her room was small, whitewashed, and colder than expected. A narrow bed stood beneath a round window. A writing desk faced the sea. Someone had placed a sprig of rowan on the pillow and a folded academy robe across the blanket. The robe was black, its cuffs unmarked. Unaligned.

Liora unpacked slowly. Two dresses. Three books. A packet of letters from neighbors who had never been farther than the market town. A tin of lavender soap wrapped in cloth. Her mother's bone comb. The iron key from Caelan, which she placed on the desk and then moved to the windowsill and then moved back to the desk because its presence seemed to change the room's balance.

When she touched the bone comb, a memory rose bright and sudden.

Elain Vale kneeling before the hearth, dark hair slipping from its braid, saying, "If anyone asks about stars, Liora, you tell them you prefer the weather."

"Why?"

"Because weather passes."

The memory faded.

Liora sat on the bed and pressed the comb to her chest.

A knock came at the door.

She opened it to find Caelan Thorne standing in the corridor without guards, jacket unbuttoned, wind-tossed and irritatingly real.

"You should not be here," Liora said.

"Because of propriety or politics?"

"Both, and possibly architecture. Does the armor know you left?"

"The armor owes me three favors."

"That raises more questions than it answers."

He glanced down the corridor. Seraphine's door was ajar by a fraction Liora suspected was deliberate. Mara's room emitted a ticking noise and an occasional spark. At the far end, Dorian's door stood shut, a line of pale ward-light glowing beneath it.

Caelan lowered his voice. "The gate called you daughter of the sealed star."

"Everyone heard."

"Not everyone knows what it means."

"Do you?"

He hesitated.

Liora gave him a flat look. "You came here to be mysterious?"

"I came here to warn you."

"That is a cousin of mysterious."

Something in his expression changed, the almost-smile lost to strain. "My father believed Elowen Vale sealed something under the North Observatory. Not a room. Not a spell. A person, or a power, or a promise. He became ill after the last time he spoke of it. Three days later, he forgot her name."

The corridor seemed to grow colder.

"Your father is the king."

"Yes."

"Kings do not forget names by accident."

"No."

Below them, the sea struck rock. Liora looked past him toward the covered bridge to the storm tower, where the animated armor stood black against moonlit glass. She wanted to shut the door. She wanted to ask everything. She wanted him to stop looking at her as if she were a door he had been waiting years to find.

"Why tell me?"

"Because when the headmistress named you fifth seat, every old family in that hall began calculating whether you are more useful alive, married, frightened, or gone."

Liora's pulse jumped at married, ridiculous and unwelcome.

Caelan saw too much. "That was not an offer."

"I did not think it was."

"You looked offended."

"I was deciding where it ranked among the threats."

The almost-smile returned, softer this time. "High, I imagine."

"Unclear. I have not met the carnivorous roses."

He looked toward the stairwell as footsteps sounded below. "Do not trust gifts. Do not answer letters sealed in white wax. Do not enter the west orchard after sunset. And if anyone mentions the Starless Compact, find me."

"What is the Starless Compact?"

"Find me faster."

The footsteps grew closer. Caelan stepped back.

Liora caught his sleeve before he could leave. She meant only to stop him. The moment her fingers touched the dark fabric, her star-sense opened with the shock of a window breaking.

She saw Caelan at twelve, drenched from rain, standing before a throne while courtiers whispered storm-cursed. She saw a woman with a silver crown cutting his palm and pressing his blood to a treaty written on skin-thin vellum. She saw him older, not much older than now, kneeling in a ruined chapel with Liora's name on his lips like a confession. She saw herself kissing him beneath falling ash. She saw herself turning away from him at a door of stars.

She released him as if burned.

Caelan's gray eyes had gone wide. "What did you see?"

"Nothing useful."

"Liora."

It was the first time he said her name. No title. No caution. Just her name, low enough to be almost private. It unsettled her more than the visions.

Seraphine's door opened fully. She stepped into the corridor wearing a silk robe and the expression of someone who had heard enough to weaponize. "Your Highness. How refreshing to discover the storm tower has developed a hallway."

Caelan straightened at once. "Ardent."

"Vale needs sleep. You need witnesses who are not me."

"I was leaving."

"Excellent. We agree on something."

He looked at Liora once more, and whatever he meant to say remained unsaid. Then he walked down the corridor, past the glow beneath Dorian's door, past the moonlit stair, and vanished onto the bridge to the storm tower.

Seraphine waited until he was gone. "Well."

Liora leaned against the doorframe. "Please do not."

"I had prepared at least twelve remarks."

"Burn them."

"Several are complimentary."

"Especially burn those."

Seraphine's teasing softened. "He looked worried."

"Everyone looks worried when they talk about my mother."

"Not about your mother."

Liora did not answer.

That night she dreamed of the North Observatory.

It stood at the highest point of Veyrfall, above even the Obsidian Spire, though no tower on the visible grounds matched it. In the dream she climbed stairs carved from black glass while snow fell upward around her. The iron key hung around her throat, warm as a pulse. At the top waited a round door sealed with seven locks. Behind it, someone sang the parish lullaby backward.

When Liora reached for the first lock, a voice spoke from the dark.

"Not yet, little star."

She turned.

Her mother stood on the stairs below, not as she had been in death, thin and fever-pale, but younger, robed in academy black with star-silver cuffs. Elowen Vale's hair was dark as Liora's, her eyes bright with the same impossible points of light.

"Mother?"

Elowen smiled with unbearable sadness. "Remember this: the academy will teach you the rules of magic, but love is older than rules. Love built the first gate. Love broke the first oath. Love will make a coward of a prince and a weapon of a parish girl if you let it."

"Are you alive?"

"That is not the question that will save you."

"What question will?"

The locks on the door began to open one by one.

Elowen looked past Liora toward the sealed dark. "Who benefits from the truth arriving late?"

Liora woke before dawn with frost on her window and the iron key lying on her pillow.

She had left it on the desk.

Across the corridor, someone screamed.

Liora threw open her door at the same time Seraphine emerged with a dagger in one hand and Mara stumbled out with Pip tangled in her hair. Dorian appeared from the far end of the corridor fully dressed, which made Liora suspect he either slept in formal wear or did not sleep at all.

"That was not me," Mara said immediately.

The scream came again from the stairwell.

They ran.

On the landing below, a first-year girl from House Auric stood frozen before the wall. The white plaster had split open in a long wet seam. Red letters oozed across it, each one forming from the wall's own stone-blood.

RETURN THE SEALED STAR OR THE PRINCE DROWNS.

Liora stopped so hard Seraphine collided with her shoulder.

The letters pulsed once.

Then every lantern in East Dormitory went out.

In the sudden dark, a hand found Liora's. She knew before the lightning flared outside the window that it was Caelan.

He had crossed from the storm tower barefoot, hair damp, breath unsteady. When lightning lit the landing, his face was pale with fury.

"This is not possible," Dorian said, voice tight. "Dormitory walls bleed only on founding anniversaries."

"You said anniversaries were real?" Liora demanded.

"Statistically."

Mara's brass kestrel clicked and projected a small beam of light from its beak. The red message shone wetly.

Caelan's hand tightened around Liora's.

She looked at their joined fingers. He did not let go. Neither did she.

Below, bells began ringing through the academy. Not breathing now. Ringing like alarms, iron and harsh.

Headmistress Veyr's voice traveled through the walls themselves. "All students remain where you are. Prefects secure the bridges. Professors to East Dormitory."

The message changed.

The red letters slid apart and reformed.

FIRST FROST COMES EARLY.

At the window, beyond the suspended lake and the black sea, snow began to fall upward from the cliffs into the starless sky.

By sunrise, Veyrfall had locked every gate.

No boats were permitted to leave the harbor. The tide bridge to the mainland folded itself into the water. The moonstone crossings between towers sealed under sheets of transparent ice. Officially, the headmistress called it a precaution after a malicious ward breach. Unofficially, every corridor carried a different version of the truth: Liora Vale had brought a curse from Bracken Parish; the prince had been marked for drowning; House Mourn had raised a funeral spell too early; Mara Venn's mechanical kestrel had pecked a forbidden rune; Seraphine Ardent had challenged a ghost to a duel and won badly.

Classes began anyway.

"Tradition," Seraphine said as they crossed the frosted courtyard toward Introduction to Practical Dangers. "Veyrfall could sink into the sea and still assign reading."

Mara, walking backward while adjusting Pip's wing, said, "If it sank, tidecraft marks would improve."

"Some of us dislike drowning."

"Skill issue."

Dorian carried three books bound in gray leather and looked more satisfied than any person should during a security crisis. "The message was composed in pre-Compact syntax. That narrows the suspect pool."

"To whom?" Liora asked.

"The elderly, the dead, and the inconveniently educated."

"That is not narrow."

"It is Veyrfall narrow."

Caelan met them at the door to the dueling hall, uniform immaculate except for the faint shadow beneath his eyes. Two royal guards stood behind him in storm-blue cloaks. They tried to follow when he joined Liora's group. He stopped, looked back, and said, "No."

The guards bowed and remained where they were, which seemed less obedience than negotiation deferred.

Seraphine watched this with open interest. "Do you often dismiss armed men with one syllable?"

"Only before breakfast."

"Efficient."

Liora tried not to remember his hand in the dark. Failed.

The dueling hall smelled of wax, iron, and old thunder. Its floor was marked with concentric circles for spell containment. Along the walls hung practice blades, storm rods, tide bowls, and veil mirrors covered in black cloth. Professor Mael stood at the center, broad-shouldered and silver-bearded, with a smile that suggested he considered student injury a form of punctuation.

"Welcome to Practical Dangers," he boomed. "If you are here, you have survived admission, supper, and the dormitory's attempt at poetry. Today we learn the first rule of power."

He pointed at Caelan.

"Your Highness. Attack me."

Caelan did not move.

Professor Mael's grin widened. "A prince who hesitates. Charming. Attack me."

Stormlight gathered around Caelan's right hand. The air sharpened. Liora felt every hair on her arms rise. He stepped forward and released a bolt of blue-white lightning straight at the professor's chest.

Professor Mael caught it in his teeth.

The class recoiled.

He swallowed. A puff of smoke escaped his ears. "The first rule of power: someone is always better at it than you."

Mara whispered, "I adore him."

"You would," Dorian said.

The lesson became a chaos of demonstrations. Seraphine cut a veil-image of herself into three shimmering duplicates, each one smiling more dangerously than the last. Dorian walked through a shadow and emerged behind a shielded student without disturbing the dust. Mara built a spring-loaded charm from two practice rings, a wire, and part of someone's hairpin, then used it to launch a sandbag into the rafters. Caelan summoned lightning again, gentler this time, shaping it into a crown before crushing it in his fist.

When Liora's turn came, the hall quieted.

Professor Mael placed a shallow black bowl before her. "Star-reading is not my art, but danger recognizes all disciplines. Look into the bowl and name the threat closest to you."

The bowl held water. Or seemed to. Its surface reflected not the ceiling but a field of stars.

Liora wanted to say the threat was the wall message, the North Observatory, the sealed star, the prince whose hand she kept feeling in hers. Instead she looked.

The stars shifted.

She saw Professor Mael's old scar ache before rain. She saw Mara's left sleeve catch fire tomorrow unless Pip intervened. She saw Seraphine laughing under a blood-red moon with tears on her face. She saw Dorian holding a white wax letter and deciding not to warn her. She saw Caelan standing at the edge of the Glassmere, water rising around his ankles though the lake hung vertical and impossible.

"Well?" Professor Mael asked.

Liora's voice came out thin. "The closest threat is choice."

The professor's brows rose.

Behind him, the bowl cracked cleanly in half.

The class ended early after that.

By noon, Liora had acquired three invitations from noble houses, two anonymous warnings, one marriage contract disguised as a scholarship offer, and a note folded into the shape of a star. The star-note contained only six words:

Ask the prince what he promised.

She found Caelan in the west orchard despite his own warning not to enter after sunset. It was afternoon, and therefore technically allowed. The orchard grew between two academy walls where sea wind could not reach. Its trees bore black apples with silver flesh. Some hung upside down from branches. Some whispered as she passed.

Caelan stood beneath the oldest tree, speaking quietly to a suit of animated armor that had followed him from the storm tower. When he saw Liora, he dismissed it with the air of someone ending a bad conversation.

"You should have guards," she said.

"You should avoid cursed orchards."

"It is daylight."

"Curses respect schedules less than prefects do."

She held up the star-note. "What did you promise?"

He went still.

The orchard whispers ceased.

"Who gave you that?"

"Someone who folds paper beautifully and threatens vaguely."

"Liora."

"Caelan."

The use of his name changed the space between them. She saw him notice. She saw herself notice him noticing and resented every layer of it.

He took the note, read it, and closed his hand until stormlight burned the paper to ash. "When I was thirteen, my father made me swear an oath on the Storm Seat. If the sealed star returned to Veyrfall, I would keep it from opening the North Observatory."

"By sealed star, you mean me."

"I did not know that then."

"But you know it now."

"Yes."

"So your warning last night was incomplete."

"Yes."

The honesty should have helped. It did not.

Black apples rustled above them. One dropped from a branch and stopped in midair between their faces, suspended by its own stubbornness.

Liora stepped closer. "Are you going to keep your oath?"

Caelan looked at her, and the stormlight in his eyes was not cold now. "I do not know."

"That is a terrible answer."

"It is the only one I have that is not a lie."

She should have left. Instead she reached for the suspended apple. Its skin was smooth and cold. When her fingers brushed it, her star-sense flared, and she saw Caelan's oath not as words but as chains of lightning wrapped around his heart. She saw the Storm Seat, a throne carved from a meteor that had fallen into the first king's battlefield. She saw the oath tighten every time he looked at her.

She let the apple fall.

It struck the ground and split open, silver flesh gleaming.

"It hurts you," she said.

Caelan's jaw tightened. "Most useful things do."

"That is the sort of sentence people say when they have been trained to mistake suffering for virtue."

For a moment, he looked less like a prince than a boy caught stealing breath. "You speak very freely to someone who could ruin you."

"You warned me I was already being calculated alive, married, frightened, or gone. Ruined was implied."

He laughed once, low and startled.

The sound did something dangerous to her heart.

Then the orchard gate slammed shut.

White frost raced across the iron bars though the sun stood high. The black apple trees bent inward. Every whisper became one voice.

FIRST FROST COMES EARLY.

From the far wall, a figure stepped out of the bark of a tree.

It wore an academy robe cut in an old style, with star-silver cuffs. Its face was hidden by a veil of white wax.

Caelan moved in front of Liora. Lightning crawled over his hands.

The figure lifted one finger and pointed at her.

"Elowen's daughter," it said. "Open what she sealed, or watch the prince drown in air."

Liora's fear sharpened into anger. "Everyone keeps asking me to open doors no one will explain."

"Explanation is for those with time."

"Then make time."

The figure tilted its wax-veiled head. "She has teeth."

"She has friends," said Seraphine from the orchard wall.

Liora looked up.

Seraphine stood balanced on the stone coping with a practice blade in one hand, cloak snapping behind her. Mara crouched beside her, Pip's brass wings spread. Dorian emerged from a shadow near the gate, expression annoyed but wand raised.

"Friends is an ambitious term," Dorian said. "Witnesses with recurring proximity."

"He means friends," Mara called.

The wax-veiled figure sighed. "Children."

Mara grinned. "Frequently underestimated. Rarely boring."

Pip launched from her shoulder. The brass kestrel streaked through the orchard, scattering sparks. Seraphine dropped from the wall in a flash of silver. Dorian pulled the figure's shadow sideways, pinning it for half a breath. Caelan struck with lightning.

The wax veil cracked.

Under it was no face, only a spinning darkness full of tiny stars.

Liora heard her mother's voice in memory: Magic always has teeth.

The figure dissolved into frost, but the frost did not fall. It streamed toward Caelan, wrapping around his throat in a translucent ring. He gasped. Lightning burst from him and struck every tree at once, filling the orchard with white fire.

Liora did not think.

She took his hand.

The star-sense opened. Not a vision this time. A map. Every point of light behind her eyes connected to another: Caelan's oath, her mother's key, the North Observatory, the Tide Gate, the suspended lake, the empty chair at high table, the wall message, the white wax letters, the dark flame in the Hall of Lanterns. At the center was a locked door made of seven names.

One name was hers.

One was Caelan's.

She spoke them together.

"Liora Vale. Caelan Thorne."

The frost ring shattered.

Caelan collapsed against her, alive and furious and breathing. She held him upright because no one else had reached them yet, because his hand had closed around hers, because when he looked down at her his expression had lost every practiced defense.

"You broke part of the oath," he said hoarsely.

"Good."

"It will punish you."

"It can get in line."

Seraphine arrived first, blade raised toward nothing. "I despise orchards."

Mara skidded in after her. "That was extraordinary. Dangerous, but excellent."

Dorian stared at Liora with open alarm. "You invoked joined names without a circle."

"Was that bad?"

"It should have killed you."

"Veyrfall has been very inconsistent about that."

Caelan was still looking at her. "Thank you."

The words were simple. Too simple for what had passed between them: oath-light, fear, breath, names braided in magic older than rules. Liora felt heat rise in her cheeks and blamed the lightning-struck trees.

Above the orchard, the academy bells began to breathe again.

Headmistress Veyr's voice followed, calm and cold through the air. "Liora Vale and Caelan Thorne, report to the Obsidian Spire. Bring the key."

Mara looked at the smoking trees. "She always knows?"

Seraphine sheathed her blade. "The woman keeps a star in a cage. I assume privacy is decorative."

Dorian adjusted his cuffs. "We should go."

"She named only us," Caelan said.

Seraphine smiled with all her teeth. "How fortunate that some of us are poor listeners."

They crossed Veyrfall under a sky where snow still fell upward. Students watched from windows and colonnades. Rumors moved faster than they did. By the time they reached the Obsidian Spire, Liora had apparently seduced the prince, cursed the prince, saved the prince, dueled the prince, and become engaged to the prince in a ceremony involving apples.

"Do rumors always improve so quickly?" she asked.

"No," Caelan said. "Usually there are more details about my tragic beauty."

She glanced at him.

He looked ahead, but his mouth curved.

At the base of the Obsidian Spire, the stone door opened without being touched. Inside, the air was black and full of stars. Stairs spiraled upward along the inner wall. No torches burned. The steps lit only when Liora set foot on them, each one brightening with a point of cold white light.

Headmistress Veyr waited at the top in a circular chamber without windows. The caged star hovered beside her. Master Quill stood near a desk piled with ledgers. Professor Mael leaned against the wall, arms folded, looking delighted by the prospect of disaster. The empty chair from high table sat in the center of the room.

"Sit," the headmistress said to Liora.

Liora did not.

Seraphine made a tiny approving sound.

Headmistress Veyr's gaze shifted to the others. "I requested two students."

"And received five," Mara said. "A bargain."

"This is not a negotiation."

"Most things are if you are annoying enough."

Professor Mael coughed into his hand.

The headmistress looked back to Liora. "Very well. Listen, all of you, and understand that what leaves this room may decide whether Veyrfall survives first frost."

Liora's fingers tightened around the iron key.

"Seventeen years ago," Headmistress Veyr said, "Elowen Vale discovered that the academy's founding spell was failing. The Glassmere, the Tide Gate, the discipline wards, even the royal oaths tied to the Storm Seat all draw from the same old bargain. We call it the Starless Compact because the founders removed its true name from the sky."

Dorian whispered, "That is impossible."

"Many facts are rude enough to be impossible."

The headmistress touched the empty chair. "Elowen believed the Compact had been altered. Someone had added a clause: when the sealed star returned, the prince of storm blood would drown, and his death would open a path beneath the North Observatory."

Caelan went very still.

"Why him?" Liora asked.

"Because royal storm blood powers the outer wards. Because grief is a key. Because old magic favors cruelty when given legal language."

The caged star pulsed.

"Elowen sealed the Observatory before the path could open. Then she fled with you. She faked her death five years later when her enemies found Bracken Parish. The body you buried was a mercy-double grown from rowan and memory."

The room tilted.

Liora sat in the empty chair because her knees had forgotten their work.

"My mother is alive."

"I believe so."

"You believe?"

"The North Observatory has been closed to me since she sealed it."

Caelan stepped beside Liora's chair, close enough that his sleeve brushed hers. Not touching. Asking without asking.

Liora stared at the key. All the grief she had carried for five years did not vanish. It changed shape, which somehow hurt more.

"Who altered the Compact?" Dorian asked.

Headmistress Veyr looked toward the dark flame visible through a narrow slit in the ceiling. "Someone inside Veyrfall. Someone with access to founding law, royal blood, and star-reading."

Master Quill whispered, "Headmistress..."

"We will not protect ghosts with politeness," Veyr said. "First frost was due in six weeks. It has come early because Liora crossed the Tide Gate. The North Observatory will open whether we prepare or not. The question is who stands at the door when it does."

The iron key warmed in Liora's palm.

Caelan lowered his voice. "You do not have to decide now."

Liora looked at him. At Seraphine with her blade-bright courage, Mara with grease on her cheek and wonder in her eyes, Dorian pretending his concern was intellectual, the headmistress who had built a life out of secrets, the prince bound by an oath that hurt him every time he chose her safety.

Love is older than rules, her mother had said.

Liora did not know if what pulled her toward Caelan was love, prophecy, danger, or the terrible intimacy of being named in the same spell. She knew only that when the academy tried to make him a sacrifice and her a key, some fierce part of her rejected both roles.

"I decide this," she said. "No one opens the Observatory through his death. No one uses my mother's work without telling me the price. And if Veyrfall wants my help, it stops treating me like a rumor with boots."

Mara grinned. Seraphine looked proud. Dorian looked as if he were adding legal objections in his head and finding none elegant enough.

Headmistress Veyr inclined her head. "A reasonable beginning."

The caged star flared.

Far above them, though no tower rose higher than the spire, a bell began to ring from a place that should not exist. Once. Twice. Seven times.

The North Observatory had heard her.

Across the room, Caelan took Liora's hand in front of professors, nobles, secrets, and the star that remembered every name.

This time, neither of them pretended it was an accident.
